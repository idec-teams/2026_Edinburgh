iDEC 2026 — Directed Evolution

One enzyme. Three activities. Cellulose to glucose.

We are engineering a single protein capable of breaking down crystalline cellulose all the way to usable glucose — by evolving a new catalytic activity into CxnA, a naturally bifunctional cellulase from Cellulomonas fimi.

Project Overview View Results
Affiliated with iDEC International Directed Evolution Competition

Two parallel tracks

What this project is doing

CxnA already does two things. We want to give it a third — and make those first two better.

Project A

Trifunctional evolution

Using directed evolution to introduce beta-glucosidase activity into the GH1 domain of CxnA. If successful, the resulting enzyme would convert cellulose all the way to glucose in a single protein, without requiring a separate beta-glucosidase.

No direct precedent exists in the literature for evolving a GH10 scaffold toward GH1-type activity. This project tests that gap directly.

In Progress epPCR MUG selection GH1 docking
Project B

Activity enhancement

Using directed evolution to improve CxnA's existing exoglucanase and endoglucanase activities — making it a more efficient cellulose-degrading enzyme overall, regardless of whether Project A succeeds.

Guided by the AlphaFold3 structural analysis and PLIP interaction maps to target residues most likely to influence substrate binding and catalytic efficiency.

In Progress epPCR CMC assay GH10 docking

Why this matters

Cellulose is the most abundant organic material on Earth. Converting it to glucose efficiently — without expensive multi-enzyme cocktails — could make biofuel production and biomass recycling significantly cheaper and more practical. This project also tests how far AI-assisted structural prediction can responsibly guide directed evolution, and where it cannot substitute for real experimental data.

How we're doing it

Dry lab + wet lab, in parallel

Structural prediction

AlphaFold3 models of the GH10 and GH1 domains, validated against the 1FHD crystal structure (RMSD 0.293 Å).

Molecular docking
