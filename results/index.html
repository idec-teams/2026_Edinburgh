Results

What we found — and what we didn't

CxnA is a two-domain enzyme: Cex (an exoglucanase) and CenA (an endoglucanase), fused together. We used error-prone PCR to scramble its DNA at random, then screened the resulting mutants to see whether any of them had picked up a useful new or improved activity. Computational predictions guided where we looked; the mutagenesis itself was random. No mutant clearly beat the unmodified enzyme — but the screening pipeline we built to find out is itself the result, and it's reusable for the next round.

Computational results

Dry Lab — where should directed evolution even look?

Before touching a single bacterium, we used structure prediction and docking software to make an educated guess at which parts of CxnA were worth targeting — and, just as importantly, which predictions we should and shouldn't trust.

DL-R1

What does CxnA actually look like?

Confirmed Methods: DL-1

CxnA has no solved crystal structure of its own, so we predicted one with ColabFold/AlphaFold2 — colouring the result by domain: Cex (exoglucanase, green), the carbohydrate-binding module that holds the two halves together (blue), and CenA (endoglucanase, pink), joined by flexible proline/threonine-rich linkers.

High confidence, within limits
pLDDT 89.3 (Cex) · 93.5 (CenA)
Both domains individually fold with high confidence. The catch: the inter-domain confidence is low (pTM 0.5; mean predicted alignment error 26.6 Å) — the linkers are floppy enough in real life that AlphaFold can't pin down how the two domains sit relative to each other. We trust the shape of each domain on its own, not the overall "pose" of the whole fusion protein.
Why does a flexible linker make the overall shape unreliable?

AlphaFold predicts confidence per residue (pLDDT) and per residue-pair (predicted alignment error, PAE). A folded domain's internal geometry is usually well constrained — there's only one way the chain can pack into a stable fold — so pLDDT is high throughout it. A flexible linker has no single correct conformation; it can be in many different positions at different moments, so the model can't confidently place the two domains it connects relative to each other, even if it's very confident about each domain individually. This is expected biology, not a modelling failure — treat the two domains as independently reliable, and the relative orientation between them as unknown.

DL-R2

Checking Cex's active site against a real crystal structure

Confirmed Methods: DL-2

Unlike the fusion as a whole, Cex alone has a published crystal structure to check against: 1FHD, a Cex-family enzyme solved with a bound inhibitor [1]. We superimposed our predicted Cex structure onto it to see how well the model's active site matches reality.

Key Result
RMSD = 0.293 Å
Cα RMSD over 1,958 aligned atoms against the 1FHD crystal structure. This is within the 0.3–0.4 Å range the original crystallographers report for two different crystal forms of Cex compared to each other — in other words, our predicted model disagrees with the real structure by no more than two real structures disagree with one another. The active-site geometry, including the catalytic residues GLU127 and GLU233, is reproduced faithfully.

Interactive 3D overlay: predicted Cex structure (teal) superimposed on the 1FHD crystal structure (orange) — Cα RMSD 0.579 Å over 311 matched pairs in this particular alignment run. Yellow spheres mark GLN87, GLU127 and GLU233. Drag to rotate; use the toggle buttons to view each structure independently.

Why this step matters

Every docking result later in this page depends on the predicted structure being trustworthy. This validation is what lets us treat the Cex model as a stand-in for the real enzyme when testing how it interacts with sugars computationally.

DL-R3

Does Cex already have a little latent β-glucosidase activity — and where would you nudge it?

Confirmed Methods: DL-3, DL-4, DL-5

Cex's fold resembles known β-glucosidase enzymes closely enough that we asked a direct question: can cellobiose — the two-sugar fragment a true β-glucosidase would split into glucose — fit into Cex's existing active site at all? We docked cellobiose into the validated Cex model with AutoDock Vina and analysed the predicted contacts with PLIP.

Top docking pose
Sits right next to the existing catalytic residues
The best-scoring pose places cellobiose in hydrogen-bonding distance of GLU127 and GLU233 — the same two residues Cex already uses to cut its natural substrate. That's consistent with the idea that Cex could be nudged toward glucosidase-like chemistry without needing a whole new active site built from scratch.

Multiple-sequence alignment against 15 homologous GH10 enzymes, cross-checked with ConSurf conservation scoring, confirmed that the catalytic motifs around GLU127 (an unusual "NEA" variant of the normal "NEP" motif) and GLU233 (the "ITELD" motif) are essentially universal across the family — strong evidence that both residues are functionally essential, not incidental.

Cex coloured by ConSurf conservation (red = most conserved). The NEA and TELD boxes mark the two catalytic motifs — both are deep red, i.e. essentially invariant across 15 homologous enzymes.

Where would you aim mutagenesis, if you were targeting it?

HotSpot Wizard flagged candidate residues near the active site that are mutable without obviously destroying the protein's fold. Those candidates were then screened computationally with FoldX (for stability) and re-docked with cellobiose (for binding). The single most promising candidate to emerge was Asn44 → Trp (ΔΔG −3.2 kcal/mol, i.e. more stable than wild type, not less).

DL-R4

CenA, the other half: where could the existing activity be improved?

Confirmed Methods: DL-3, DL-4, DL-5

CenA (the endoglucanase half of CxnA, family GH6) already works — the Project B goal isn't to invent a new activity but to make an existing one better. We docked cellotetraose (a four-sugar cellulose fragment) into CenA's active site and confirmed contacts with its three catalytic aspartates: Asp595 (acid catalyst), Asp631 (stabilises the acid catalyst's protonation state) and Asp771 (base catalyst).

CenA coloured by ConSurf conservation across 41 homologous GH6 sequences (magenta = most conserved, teal = most variable). 13 of the 27 residues contacting the docked substrate are fully conserved — a strong signal of functional importance.

As with Cex, candidate mutations were screened by combining FoldX stability predictions with re-docking scores — this time checking both metrics together, plus whether the catalytic geometry held up across repeated modelling runs.

DL-R5

An important honesty note: prediction guided priorities, it didn't dictate the mutants

Context

Read this before the wet-lab results

N44W and K671S are the dry lab's top computational candidates — but the actual wet-lab mutant library wasn't built by inserting those specific changes. It was built with error-prone PCR, which introduces mutations randomly across the gene. The computational work told us the Cex and CenA active sites were worth targeting and gave us a reference point for interpreting what random mutagenesis turned up — it didn't guarantee any particular mutation would actually appear in the library. Keep that distinction in mind in the section below: none of the sequenced wet-lab mutants carry N44W or K671S.

Experimental results

Wet Lab — building and screening real mutants

Three screens, run in sequence, each narrowing down which of the random mutants were worth a closer look: a visual test for endoglucanase activity, a colour test for glucosidase activity, and finally a quantitative fluorescence assay we had to build and validate from scratch.

WL-R1

The plasmid, and an uninvited stowaway

Confirmed Methods: WL-1, WL-2

CxnA was carried on a pSB1C3 plasmid backbone with a lac promoter and ribosome binding site ahead of the insert. Transforming E. coli with this construct retained both exo- and endo-glucanase activity, confirming the starting enzyme was functional before any mutagenesis began.

Plasmid map of the CxnA construct. CxnA itself is only two domains — Cex (exoglucanase) and CenA (endoglucanase) — joined through a carbohydrate-binding module (CBD2). The duplicated chloramphenicol-resistance region on the right was an unplanned discovery (below).

Unplanned finding
An extra ~1 kb of DNA in the backbone
Gel electrophoresis and sequencing turned up an insert in the backbone that wasn't supposed to be there. A BLASTp search against the translated sequence matched it to a duplicated chloramphenicol-resistance pseudogene — most likely a quirk of this specific plasmid stock rather than anything we introduced. It doesn't appear to have blocked cloning or expression, but its effect on plasmid stability wasn't formally tested.
WL-R2

Building the mutant library — smaller than planned

Confirmed Methods: WL-3

Error-prone PCR (epPCR) was applied only to the CxnA insert, using manganese chloride to deliberately lower the copying polymerase's accuracy — the backbone, promoter and ribosome binding site were amplified separately with a high-fidelity polymerase so mutations wouldn't land somewhere that would confound the activity readout. We ran this on two versions of the gene in parallel: the native sequence, and a codon-optimised version ordered as synthetic DNA blocks (to work around the gene's unusually high, amplification-hostile GC content — 71%).

Honest limitation
24 (codon-optimised) and 15 (native) colonies
Both libraries came out markedly smaller than planned — the GC content made PCR temperamental, and several technical issues (inconsistent mixing, an unexpectedly-migrating linearised plasmid control) slowed troubleshooting. A small library means fewer chances for a rare beneficial mutation to be sampled at all; this is the single biggest constraint on everything that follows.
WL-R3

Screen 1 — Congo red: did endoglucanase activity survive?

Confirmed Methods: WL-4

Congo red dye binds intact cellulose (here, the related polymer CMC) but washes off wherever an active endoglucanase has chewed it up — so a colony with working CenA activity shows up as a clear halo on a red background. This was run first, as a cheap way to immediately discard any mutant that had simply broken the protein.

Congo red screen. (A) All 15 codon-optimised mutants, numbered. (B) All 15 native-sequence mutants (only 15 total colonies were available in that library). A clear halo = endoglucanase activity retained.

Result
7 of 15 native, 15 of 15 codon-optimised retained a halo
All 15 codon-optimised mutants showed some clearing, at varying sizes. Of the native mutants, colony 4 had the largest halo (closest to wild type); colonies 12, 14 and 15 showed no clearing at all — a likely sign that their mutation broke the protein outright, not a subtle activity change. All five of these were flagged for sequencing.
WL-R4

Screen 2 — X-gluc: any sign of new glucosidase activity?

Confirmed Methods: WL-5

X-gluc is a colourless compound that turns a colony visibly blue if the cell is producing β-glucosidase-like activity. Every mutant from both libraries — 30 colonies total — was screened this way in parallel with Congo red, alongside controls: a known β-glucosidase (positive control), wild-type CxnA, and a non-functional His-tagged CxnA (negative control).

Controls for both screens. Left: Congo red. Right: X-gluc — note that wild-type CxnA (top right spot) already shows a faint blue tint, not just the positive-control β-glucosidase (bottom left).

X-gluc screen on all 30 mutants. (A) codon-optimised. (B) native sequence.

Unexpected background signal
Wild-type CxnA already shows faint baseline activity
The non-functional His-tagged negative control looked the same as wild-type CxnA — both showed a faint blue tint beyond the truly blank background. This matches the dry-lab prediction (DL-R3) that Cex might already have some latent capacity to engage cellobiose-like substrates. No mutant came close to the true positive control, but several (native colonies 4, 14, 15; codon-optimised colonies 2, 4, 7, 12) were darker than the wild-type baseline and were flagged for sequencing and quantitative follow-up.
WL-R5

Screen 3 — putting a number on it: the MUC fluorescence assay

Confirmed Methods: WL-6 · Full troubleshooting: Lab Notebook, Aug–Sep

Congo red and X-gluc are useful for sorting mutants into rough buckets, but neither gives a number you can actually compare between colonies. To measure exoglucanase activity precisely, we used MUC — a substrate that releases a fluorescent molecule (4-MU) in proportion to how much active enzyme is present. Getting trustworthy numbers out of this assay took six rounds of calibration (the full story — including a substrate mix-up that briefly looked like a real signal — is in the lab notebook); the validated version is summarised below.

Establishing a trustworthy assay. (A) Fluorescence is only proportional to 4-MU concentration below 6.25 µM — above that, the reading undershoots for optical reasons, not biology, so all rates were fit only within the reliable range. (B) Adding more lysate gave progressively less signal per microlitre — a volume artefact, not reduced activity. (C) Fixed by titrating active lysate against inactive (frameshift) lysate instead, which restored a straight-line relationship at two different temperatures/settings. (D) No signal from substrate, medium, or host alone — the frameshift control sits at just 0.7% of induced CxnA.

With the assay validated, we quantified exoglucanase activity for the seven mutants flagged by the earlier screens, each compared against a wild-type control grown and lysed alongside it on the same day.

Activity of all seven flagged mutants, relative to a same-day wild-type control (bars = median of 3 technical replicates; whiskers = range). The shaded band marks how much two colonies of the same, unmutated genotype normally differ from each other just from day-to-day prep variation — only a bar that clears this band by itself is a plausible real effect.

The headline number
9.5% colony-to-colony noise → need ≥1.40× to call it real
Before trusting any "improved" mutant, we first measured how much two colonies of the identical, unmutated wild type differ from each other purely from everyday variability in growth and lysis. The answer was 9.5%. Statistically, that means a single mutant colony needs to beat wild type by at least 1.40× before we can call the difference real rather than noise (a "3σ" threshold, in the jargon).

We also tried to run this as a MUG assay

The same lysates, prepared and assayed exactly the same way, were also tested with MUG — the equivalent substrate for β-glucosidase activity, which is what Project A is actually trying to evolve. Every sample lit up immediately, including the dead-enzyme frameshift control. The reason turned out to be simple: the bottle on the shelf was the wrong substrate — a β-galactosidase substrate (MUGal), not the β-glucosidase one (MUG) — and E. coli TOP10 carries enough background β-galactosidase machinery to hydrolyse it regardless of what CxnA was doing. The MUG assay was dropped once this was confirmed. It doesn't affect the MUC results above — the dead frameshift control sits at under 1% signal there, so this background enzyme clearly doesn't touch the cellobioside substrate. Full troubleshooting: 15 September notebook entry.

WL-R6

What mutations were actually there?

Confirmed Methods: WL-7

Six colonies flagged by the screens above were miniprepped and sent for Sanger sequencing to find out what had actually changed in their DNA.

A sobering finding
Most sequenced colonies carried no mutations at all
Several of the sequenced codon-optimised colonies came back identical to the parent sequence — epPCR simply hadn't introduced a change in those particular colonies by chance. That's actually informative: if "mutants" with zero genetic change still show a 0.57–1.39× spread in measured activity, that spread has to be prep-to-prep noise, not genetics — which is exactly the kind of variation the 1.40× statistical threshold above was designed to filter out.

The clearest exception was colony T4 (the one that showed strongly attenuated activity, 0.16× wild type). Its sequencing surfaced two point mutations: a glycine → aspartic acid change in the exoglucanase (Cex) domain, and an aspartic acid → glycine change in the endoglucanase (CenA) domain. Because CxnA's two active sites sit several hundred residues apart, losing both activities from two separate, unrelated point mutations landing simultaneously is a coincidence we'd rather not have to explain — a single misfolding event destabilising the whole fusion protein is the simpler explanation, and fits the data better.

WL-R7

So — did it work?

The honest answer

No mutant demonstrated a confirmed improvement in activity. CO12 is a genuine candidate for a future round — it's the only variant that beat wild type at all, and its 1.35× average sits right at the edge of our detection limit — but it doesn't clear the bar for statistical confidence yet, and more replicates are needed before calling it a real hit rather than a lucky draw. Two mutants (T14, T15) appear to have lost function entirely, most likely through protein misfolding rather than a targeted change to either active site.

What did work: we built a complete, repeatable pipeline — random mutagenesis, two fast qualitative screens, a validated quantitative fluorescence assay, and sequencing to close the loop — that took a library from "30 random colonies" down to "one statistically-interesting candidate" in a single round. The library was small and the GC-rich gene was a genuine technical obstacle throughout; a larger library run through the same pipeline is the obvious next step, not a different approach.

Why we're reporting a null result at all

A "nothing improved, but here's exactly why and how confident we are" result is a real scientific outcome, not a failure to hide. Every negative or inconclusive finding on this page — the collapsed T14/T15 mutants, the unconfirmed CO12 candidate, the small library sizes — is reported as measured, not rounded up or omitted.
