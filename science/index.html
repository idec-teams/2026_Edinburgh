Background Science

Cellulose, enzymes, and directed evolution

The biology behind this project — from cellulose's molecular structure to how we use laboratory evolution to engineer new protein functions.

The substrate

What is cellulose, and why is it hard to break down?

Cellulose is the structural polymer that makes up plant cell walls. Chemically, it is a linear chain of glucose molecules linked by β-1,4 glycosidic bonds — the same building blocks as starch, but connected at a different angle. That seemingly small chemical difference is what makes cellulose so recalcitrant.

Where starch forms loose, helical coils that are easily digested, cellulose chains pack tightly into hydrogen-bonded, crystalline microfibrils. This crystalline structure is extraordinarily stable — it resists most chemical and enzymatic attack, which is why wood is strong and cotton doesn't dissolve in water.

To fully convert cellulose to glucose requires breaking through multiple levels of structural complexity: first loosening the crystalline packing, then fragmenting the chains, then cleaving the resulting disaccharide cellobiose into free glucose. Each step requires a different enzyme class, acting synergistically.

Why does the β-1,4 linkage matter so much?

The β configuration at the glycosidic bond causes each glucose unit to rotate 180° relative to its neighbour, producing an extended, flat ribbon rather than a helix. These flat ribbons can stack and hydrogen-bond to each other very efficiently, forming the tight crystalline bundles that give cellulose its mechanical strength.

Animals lack the enzymes (cellulases) to break β-1,4 bonds — which is why cows, termites, and other cellulose-eating animals rely on gut microbes that do produce cellulases. Fungi and bacteria such as Cellulomonas fimi (the source organism of CxnA) are among the most efficient natural cellulose degraders known.

The enzyme system

Three enzyme classes — all required, acting in concert

No single natural enzyme can convert crystalline cellulose to glucose alone. A minimum of three activities are required, and they are synergistic — each one makes the others more effective.

GH10 / Cex

Exoglucanase

Present in CxnA WT

Attacks crystalline cellulose from the non-reducing ends of chains, processively releasing the disaccharide cellobiose. Particularly important for initiating degradation of the crystalline fraction.

Structural and mechanistic detail

The Cex (exoglucanase) domain of CxnA belongs to glycoside hydrolase family GH10. GH10 enzymes use a retaining double-displacement mechanism, with two conserved glutamate residues (GLU127 as nucleophile and GLU233 as acid/base in this project's structural model) coordinating catalysis. [3]

The active-site tunnel topology of exoglucanases enables processive movement along the cellulose chain, explaining their preference for crystalline rather than amorphous substrates.

GH10 / CenA

Endoglucanase

Present in CxnA WT

Cleaves β-1,4 bonds at internal positions within the cellulose chain — not at the ends. This creates new chain ends that the exoglucanase can then attack, making the two activities strongly synergistic.

Structural and mechanistic detail

CenA-type endoglucanases have an open active-site cleft (rather than a tunnel), which enables them to bind and cleave internal bonds. CxnA uniquely combines Cex and CenA activities in a single polypeptide, which is the starting point for this project. [1]

GH1 / BGL

Beta-glucosidase

Project A target

Converts the disaccharide cellobiose into two glucose molecules. This is the final step in converting cellulose to fermentable sugar, and the activity this project is attempting to evolve into CxnA.

Structural and mechanistic detail

GH1 family beta-glucosidases also use a retaining mechanism, with two glutamate residues (catalytic nucleophile and acid/base). In this project's structural model, GLU350 and GLU404 are identified as candidate positions. [4]

A key finding from the docking analysis is the steric barrier formed by GLN87 and TRP281, which may limit cellobiose access to the active site. Understanding and potentially overcoming this barrier is central to Project A's strategy. See the Results page for full discussion.

Our starting enzyme

CxnA: an engineered bifunctional cellulase

CxnA isn't a protein nature produces on its own. It was engineered by Duedu & French at the University of Edinburgh in 2016 [1], by fusing the catalytic domains of two separate cellulose-degrading enzymes naturally produced by Cellulomonas fimi — a Gram-positive soil bacterium known for an unusually effective natural cellulose-degradation system — into a single polypeptide chain.

What makes CxnA notable is that the fusion genuinely works: the resulting protein shows both exoglucanase and endoglucanase activity in one chain, something most organisms only achieve by deploying two separate enzymes. This project asks whether the same strategy can go one step further — not by adding a third domain, but by evolving a third activity directly into one of the two domains CxnA already has.

CxnA has exactly two domains: Cex (family GH10, exoglucanase) and CenA (family GH6, endoglucanase), joined through a carbohydrate-binding module (CBM). There is no third, pre-existing domain. The directed-evolution target in this project is to evolve new β-glucosidase-like activity directly within Cex's own active site — its fold resembles known β-glucosidases closely enough to make this a plausible, if unprecedented, target.

Key datum: Structural validation

The AlphaFold3 model of the GH10/Cex domain was validated against the published crystal structure 1FHD (Notenboom et al., 1998 [3]). RMSD = 0.293 Å over aligned Cα atoms — indicating high structural accuracy and supporting the reliability of downstream docking predictions. See Results for full validation analysis.

The method

What is directed evolution?

A laboratory technique that mimics natural selection — but compressed into weeks, and directed toward a specific functional goal.

1

Introduce random mutations

Error-prone PCR (epPCR) amplifies the target gene with a deliberately elevated error rate, creating a library of thousands of sequence variants — each a slightly different version of the protein. This is analogous to natural mutation, but we control the rate.

2

Apply selection pressure

The mutant library is expressed in bacteria, which are then challenged with a condition that only cells expressing the desired activity can survive or grow well under. For Project A: growth on cellobiose as sole carbon source. For Project B: enhanced zone-clearing on CMC plates.

3

Screen survivors

Colonies that grow are screened with fluorogenic substrates (MUG for beta-glucosidase, MUC for exoglucanase) to quantify activity. False positives — cells that grew for reasons unrelated to the target activity — are identified and eliminated.

4

Characterise and iterate

Hits are sequenced to identify which mutations confer the activity, then validated in purified enzyme assays. The best variants can then be subjected to another round of mutagenesis — iterating toward the desired function.

Why directed evolution works

Natural evolution explores protein sequence space over millions of generations and years. Directed evolution recapitulates this process in the lab by: deliberately narrowing the sequence space explored (we start from a protein that already has related structure), applying strong artificial selection pressure (survival on a specific substrate), and iterating rapidly (weeks, not millennia).

The key insight is that you don't need to rationally design every mutation. The selection/screening step acts as the filter — you just need to generate enough sequence diversity that beneficial mutations exist somewhere in the library, and then find them.

This project also uses computational structural analysis (AlphaFold3 + docking) to guide the mutagenesis — targeting epPCR to the regions most likely to be functionally important, reducing the haystack that selection must search.

Important limitation

Computational predictions guide but cannot replace experimental selection. Docking scores predict approximate binding geometry; they do not predict whether a mutation will actually confer catalytic activity in a living cell. All computational findings in this project are treated as hypotheses to be tested in the wet lab, not as conclusions.

Scientific foundation

Key literature this project builds on

2016

Duedu & French — CxnA characterisation

The original description of CxnA as a bifunctional cellulase from Cellulomonas fimi. Established the dual GH10 domain activity and provided the sequence and expression system this project uses as its starting point.

Role in this project: Provides the wild-type CxnA baseline — gene sequence, expression protocol, confirmed activities, and domain architecture.
1998/2000

Notenboom et al. — GH10/Cex crystal structure

Crystal structure determination of the Cex exoglucanase domain (PDB: 1FHD and related entries), establishing the active-site architecture including the catalytic glutamates. [3]

Role in this project: Provides the gold-standard crystal structure used to validate AlphaFold3 predictions (RMSD 0.293 Å). Establishes GLU127 and GLU233 as the catalytic dyad.
2018

Liu et al. — GH1 beta-glucosidase structure/function

Structural and functional characterisation of a GH1 family beta-glucosidase, including the catalytic mechanism and key active-site residues. [4]

Role in this project: Informs identification of candidate catalytic residues (GLU350, GLU404) in the GH1-fold domain of CxnA for Project A.
